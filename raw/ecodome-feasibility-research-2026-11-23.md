# 50-Foot Multimedia Geodome Business: Feasibility Research

## 1. Summary

A 50-foot geodesic dome gives roughly 1,960 sq ft of floor area and an apex of about 25 ft. Published capacity figures range from about 150 to 200 seated and 260 to 300 standing, depending on layout and the local fire official. It is a credible size for both private events and ticketed immersive shows.

The most important finding is that **the dome shell is the smaller part of the investment**. Because the dome on offer is frame and cover only, the business still needs a projection liner and negative-pressure system, a multi-projector system with warp/blend software, audio, power, flooring, climate control, transport, and lifting equipment. Benchmarks suggest the multimedia layer will cost **more than the dome itself**, likely $100,000 to $250,000+ depending on quality tier (see Section 3).

There appears to be **no local operator of large geodesic event/projection domes in central Virginia**. The nearest direct competitors are national touring companies (DomeGuys, Pacific Domes, NewMedia, Lumen & Forge, SoFlo Studio), which charge travel and crew costs on top of rental. Indirect competitors are strong, however: the Science Museum of Virginia rents a 250-seat dome theater from about $2,500, Exhibition Hub runs 360° immersive art shows in Henrico, and Charlottesville has established tent companies serving the wedding market.

The hybrid model has a built-in tension: every week the dome is installed as a ticketed venue is a week it cannot be rented out. Also, a structure standing for 180 days or more stops being a "temporary tent" under Virginia code and must meet permanent building code. A seasonal venue run (for example, spring and fall weekends at a host site) with mobile rentals in between is the most workable version.

**Profit is plausible but highly sensitive to bookings.** In the illustrative model (Section 9), the mobile side loses money at about 8 projection bookings a year, roughly breaks even to modest profit around 15, and becomes attractive at 25+. The seasonal ticketed run depends almost entirely on occupancy.

### Critical next steps before buying the dome

1. Get the dome's manufacturer, model, year, engineering/wind report, and NFPA 701 flame certificate and label. Confirm whether the cover is translucent or blackout (projection needs blackout).
2. Meet the Albemarle County building official and fire marshal (or whichever county is the home base) with the dome specs, to confirm permit path, occupant load, and that digital projection inside a membrane structure is acceptable (see Section 5.3).
3. Get a projection design and quote from a dome AV integrator before committing; it is the largest cost line.
4. Get insurance quotes from a broker who writes tent/event-rental programs.
5. Test demand: line up two or three anchor clients (a winery, a corporate client, a festival, a school division) with letters of intent.

### 2.3 Questions to ask the seller

- Manufacturer, model, year of manufacture, and number of times erected.
- Engineering report (wind and snow ratings) and whether it is stamped for any U.S. state. Virginia officials may ask for it.
- NFPA 701 / CPAI-84 flame certificate for the cover, and whether the permanent fabric label is intact. Virginia requires a permanently affixed label identifying size and fabric type.
- Cover type: translucent, opaque, or blackout. Any tears, patches, mildew, or UV degradation.
- Complete inventory: struts, hubs, bolts, doors, anchor plates, ballast attachments, assembly manual.
- Whether the manufacturer still supports the model (replacement struts, a matching projection liner).

## 3. Making It "Multimedia": Projection, Audio, and Show Systems

### 3.1 Why a liner matters

A projected image directly on a geodesic cover shows the frame and fabric wrinkles. SoFlo Studio states plainly that its standard projection add-on is without a smooth screen and may show imperfections, with a seamless screen at extra cost. Professional dome operators use a **negative-pressure liner**: a fan pulls air from the gap between the outer cover and an inner fabric screen, so the screen is drawn smoothly toward the frame. Fulldome.pro describes this as the now-common lower-cost alternative to rigid aluminum planetarium screens, and DomeGuys and Pacific Domes both sell liners for their geodesic domes.

### 3.2 Projector system

A 50-foot dome is too large for the single-projector fisheye kits used in small inflatable planetariums (those are typically sized for domes up to about 6 meters). A 15-meter dome needs several laser projectors edge-blended and warped into one image. Reference points:

- Christie markets its 15,750-lumen and 25,300-lumen laser models for mid-sized domes.
- Zeiss uses standard video projectors in multi-channel fulldome layouts, with "4K" fulldome systems typically delivering roughly 12 to 15 million effective pixels.
- An industry rule of thumb published by Blooloop is to target about 9 to 10 foot-lamberts of brightness for 2D content; audiences prefer a bright 4K dome to a dim 8K one.
- A typical rental-grade large-venue laser projector (Epson Pro L1755U, 15,000 lumens) listed at $33,000 and has been seen discounted to around $22,400; Epson has since replaced it with the 16,000-lumen EB-PU2216B. Lenses are sold separately.

The exact projector count, brightness, and lens choice must come from a dome projection design, not from a brochure. **Estimate** for planning: 5 to 6 laser projectors in the 10,000 to 16,000 lumen class.

### 3.4 Content

Content is a recurring cost and a major creative decision.

- **Licensed fulldome shows** (astronomy, science, nature, music): small venues pay modest fees. For example, Loch Ness Productions prices a one-year license for one show at $2,000 for venues under 5,000 annual attendance and $4,000 for under 10,000. Larger distributors (Cosm/Evans & Sutherland, Zeiss, AMNH, Adler) price by dome size and attendance.
- **Free/attribution content** exists (for example, NOIRLab's archive) but commercial use is often restricted. Check every license.
- **Custom content** for corporate clients and brand activations is billed to the client and is where turnkey companies make much of their margin. She will need either an in-house motion designer or a contract studio.
- **Music** played at public, ticketed events usually requires public-performance licensing (ASCAP, BMI, and others). Confirm with a broker or attorney.

## 4. Logistics and Manpower

### 4.1 Setup and strike time

| Source | Build time for a 50' dome | Crew |
|---|---|---|
| Pacific Domes 50' spec sheet | ~14 hours | 4 |
| DomeGuys project write-up | ~12–14 hours | 5 |
| Lumen & Forge (large projection domes, 50–120 ft) | ~20–50 hours | Not stated |
| SoFlo Studio | Plan for site access 3–4 days before and after the event | Not stated |

The 12 to 14 hour figures are for the **structure only**, by experienced crews. Add time for the projection liner, rigging and aiming projectors, warp/blend calibration, audio, HVAC, flooring, and egress lighting. A realistic plan for a projection event is **2 to 3 days of install and 1 to 2 days of strike**. DomeGuys says its own crews build in about half the time of inexperienced crews, so the first several builds will be slower.

### 4.2 Crew roles

| Role | When | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Crew chief / structure lead | Every install | Must know the build sequence, anchoring, and inspection checklist |
| General build crew (3–4) | Install and strike | Can be trained local hires or a labor agency; must be on payroll or covered for workers' comp |
| Equipment operator | Install and strike | Telehandler / lift operation; check OSHA training requirements |
| Projection / AV technician | Install, show days, strike | The scarcest skill; calibration quality makes or breaks the product |
| Show operator | Each show or event | Runs playback, audio, lighting; can be the AV tech for small events |
| Front-of-house / ushers (ticketed) | Each show day | Count occupancy, manage egress, ticket scanning |
| Sales and event manager | Ongoing | Site visits, permits, client coordination, contracts |
| Content producer (contract) | As needed | Custom fulldome content for corporate clients |

Turnkey dome companies outsource some of this too. DomeGuys offers three models: its own full crew; a project manager who supervises the client's crew and equipment; or shipping the dome with instructions for the client's crew. Pacific Domes lists setup-and-strike service for a 50' dome at $16,500, with 4 staff and a 10,000-lb reach forklift, excluding shipping, equipment, and staff travel. That is a useful benchmark both for what a local operator might charge and for what it might cost to hire experienced help for the first few builds.

### 4.3 Site requirements for every booking

- Level ground or hard surface, with room around the dome for the crew, lift, and delivery vehicle.
- Anchoring method approved for the surface (stakes in soil, ballast on pavement), per the dome's engineering report.
- Utilities marked before any staking ("Miss Utility" / VA811).
- Power: generator or shore power sized for projectors, audio, HVAC, and the negative-pressure fan.
- Fire department access, exit paths, and separation distances (Section 5).
- Weather plan: wind limits for occupancy and for working at height, lightning procedures, and a decision rule for closing the dome.

### 4.4 Seasonality in central Virginia

Hot, humid summers make cooling essential for an enclosed, dark projection dome. Winter makes heating, snow load, and ice on the cover concerns. The strongest outdoor months are typically spring and fall, which is also peak wedding and festival season. Indoor placements (for example, in a large arena or convention hall) avoid weather but require ceiling height above ~25 ft plus rigging clearance.

## 5. Permits and Regulatory Requirements (Virginia)

### 5.1 Tent / membrane structure permits

Virginia's statewide codes treat a fabric-covered dome as a tent or membrane structure:

- **Building permit:** the Virginia Construction Code exempts only tents of 900 sq ft or less with an occupant load of 50 or fewer. A 50' dome (~1,960 sq ft) **always** needs a permit, every time it is erected.
- **Operational (fire) permit:** the local fire official may also require one under the Statewide Fire Prevention Code. Albemarle County's own tent guideline says structures over 900 sq ft or 50 occupants need a building permit from Community Development.
- **Plans:** applications with an occupant load of 50 or more must include a site and floor plan showing exits, seating, and the location of heating and electrical equipment.
- **No occupancy before final inspection approval.** The permittee must also inspect the structure at regular intervals while it stands.

### 5.2 The 180-day line (critical for the venue side)

Under Virginia code, tents and membrane structures erected for **less than 180 days** are temporary and follow the fire code's tent chapter. If erected for **longer than 180 days**, the structure is no longer a temporary tent and must comply with the full Virginia Construction Code, including fire protection systems, egress, and accessibility. A year-round fixed venue in the dome is therefore a much bigger regulatory and cost undertaking than a seasonal run. Separately, Virginia's fire code requires membrane structures over 7,500 sq ft to meet the building code; a 50' dome is well under that.

### 5.3 Key fire-code provisions to plan around

- **Exits:** at least two exits for 10 to 199 occupants (more above that), each at least 72 inches wide, spaced around the perimeter, with no point more than 100 ft from an exit. Public aisles at least 44 inches. Exit signs and egress lighting while occupied.
- **Materials:** the cover, liner, drapes, and floor coverings must meet NFPA 701 flame-propagation criteria (or be approved flame-retardant treated).
- **Prohibited:** smoking, open flames, fireworks within 100 ft, flammable-liquid-fueled equipment inside, and combustible materials such as hay or straw in assembly use.
- **Separation:** generators at least 20 ft away; cooking and heating equipment at least 10 ft from exits; LP-gas containers outside and set back; combustible vegetation cleared 30 ft around.
- **Projection clause to clarify:** the DHCD overview of the tent rules lists the display of motion pictures in tents as prohibited except for "safety film." This language dates from the era of flammable film stock, and digital projection is used in event domes nationwide, but **the project should get the local fire official's written confirmation** that digital projection is acceptable before booking a show.

### 5.4 Zoning

Zoning applies wherever the dome is placed, and it matters most for the ticketed-venue side.

- In Albemarle County, weddings, receptions, and other event-space rentals on Rural Areas-zoned land require a **special use permit**, except at bona fide farm wineries, breweries, and distilleries.
- A county guidance document put the special use permit cost at about $2,500 and the review at a minimum of 6 months.
- Farm wineries, breweries, and distilleries can host some events by right, but events over 200 attendees require a special use permit, and there are minimum acreage rules (a summary of the county rules cites 21 acres for agritourism events at these sites).
- **Practical implication:** partnering with an existing permitted venue (a winery, brewery, event farm, or a commercially zoned site in Charlottesville or Richmond) is far faster than permitting land the project controls. Confirm with county zoning whether a ticketed dome show at a farm winery counts as an allowed event and how attendance is counted.
- Each other county or city has its own rules; Richmond-area localities will differ.

### 5.5 Business registration and other items

- Business entity (LLC is common), Virginia SCC registration, local business license (BPOL), and sales tax registration if the business sells taxable items. Ask an accountant whether ticket sales and equipment rentals are taxable locally.
- Commercial driver and vehicle rules if the truck and trailer combination is heavy enough.
- Alcohol: if events serve alcohol, the caterer or venue usually holds the Virginia ABC license, and liquor liability coverage is needed.
- Accessibility: ADA access to the entrance and seating for ticketed public shows.

## 6. Insurance

### 6.1 Core policies

| Coverage | Why it matters | Typical requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial general liability | Third-party injury or property damage from the structure or events | Venues and municipalities commonly ask for $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate, with a certificate of insurance naming them as additional insured |
| Inland marine (equipment floater) | Covers the dome and AV gear in storage, in transit, and set up on site. Standard property policies often do not | Insure at replacement value; confirm wind and theft coverage while installed |
| Commercial auto | Truck and trailer, plus hired and non-owned auto | Required for any company vehicle |
| Workers' compensation | Install crews face falls, lifting, and equipment injuries | **Required in Virginia once a business regularly employs more than two people (part-time counts), and subcontractors' employees count toward that total** |
| Umbrella / excess liability | Large crowds and structural failures can exceed primary limits | Often $1M–$5M; some clients will require it |
| Liquor liability | If alcohol is served at the operator's own ticketed events | Often $1M per occurrence |
| Event cancellation / weather | Protects ticket revenue and deposits on the operator's own shows | Optional; weigh cost against risk |
| Professional / E&O | If the business designs content or produces events for clients | Optional, but useful for production services |

### 6.2 Points to raise with a broker

- Many general liability policies exclude or limit "amusement devices," aerial work, or temporary structures. Confirm the policy explicitly covers erecting and dismantling a geodesic structure.
- Ask whether the inland marine policy covers the dome while it is in a client's possession and during high-wind events.
- Clients (universities, municipalities, corporations) will each have their own certificate and additional-insured requirements. UVA, for example, keeps pre-certified tent vendors on file for stage permits, which suggests institutional clients expect documented compliance.
- Premiums depend on revenue, payroll, claims history, and limits. **Budget estimate only:** $12,000–$25,000 per year for a combined package in the first year, to be replaced with real quotes.

## 9. Illustrative Profit Model

**All figures in this section are planning estimates for discussion, not research findings.** They exist to show which variables matter most. Replace them with real quotes and local market feedback.

### 9.1 Mobile rentals (dome + projection events)

Assumptions: average price $20,000 per projection event; direct cost per event about $9,500 (5-person crew for 3 days, AV tech and operator for 4 days, telehandler and lift rental, transport, consumables, permit fees); annual fixed costs about $70,000 (insurance, storage, vehicle, marketing, content licenses, maintenance, accounting); equipment depreciation about $40,000 per year (about $200,000 of AV and dome assets over 5 years).

| Scenario | Events / year | Revenue | Contribution after direct costs | Less fixed + depreciation | Operating profit before owner pay |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 8 | $160,000 | $84,000 | $110,000 | **–$26,000** |
| Base | 15 | $300,000 | $157,500 | $110,000 | **$47,500** |
| High | 25 | $500,000 | $262,500 | $110,000 | **$152,500** |

Dome-shell-only bookings (weddings, tented parties) would add smaller but more frequent revenue on top of this.

### 9.2 Seasonal ticketed run at a host venue

Assumptions: 12 weekends per season (well under the 180-day limit), 3 show days per weekend, 6 shows per day, 120 seats per show (25,920 seats per season), $22 average net ticket. Variable costs of 21% (6% ticketing and payment fees plus a 15% revenue share to the host). Season fixed costs about $107,000 (front-of-house and operator staffing, marketing, content licenses, power and HVAC, install/strike at the host site, added insurance).

| Occupancy | Tickets sold | Revenue | After variable costs | Less season fixed costs | Season profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25% | 6,480 | $142,600 | $112,600 | $107,000 | **~$5,600** |
| 50% | 12,960 | $285,100 | $225,200 | $107,000 | **~$118,000** |
| 75% | 19,440 | $427,700 | $337,900 | $107,000 | **~$231,000** |

### 9.3 What the model tells us

- **Utilization is everything.** Fixed costs and depreciation are large, so the business only works with a steady booking calendar.
- **The two halves compete for the same asset.** A 12-weekend venue season removes those weekends from the rental calendar, often during peak wedding and festival months. Scheduling the ticketed season in shoulder periods (late winter, early summer, holidays) reduces this conflict.
- **AV quality drives price.** A dim or seamed image will not support the higher package prices.
- **Starting capital** for a professional launch is likely in the **$250,000 to $400,000** range once the dome purchase, multimedia layer, vehicle, ballast, and working capital are included. A leaner launch (dome shell rentals first, then adding projection, or renting projection gear per event) could cut this substantially at the cost of lower early pricing.

## 10. Other Factors to Consider

- **Phased launch.** Start with dome-shell rentals (weddings, parties, corporate) and rent projection gear per event from an AV house, then buy the projection system once demand is proven.
- **Partnerships.** Local tent companies for flooring, HVAC, power, and labor; a winery or brewery as a host venue; a planetarium or university for educational content; an AV integrator for design and calibration.
- **Skills gap.** Projection calibration and fulldome content are specialized. The first hire or partner should be a strong projection technician.
- **Weather and wind policy.** Define in writing when the dome is closed to occupants, and when the crew stops working at height.
- **Maintenance.** Cover cleaning and storage (dry, rolled properly to avoid mildew), strut and bolt inspection, projector filter and calibration checks, fan and liner care.
- **Resale value.** Domes and pro projectors retain resale value, which limits downside if the business does not work.
- **Market growth.** One fulldome software publisher cites industry estimates of about $3.8 billion in immersive dome projection in 2025, growing around 9.5% per year. This is a third-party market-research figure, useful as context but not proof of local demand.
- **Business models to learn from.** Lunarhive's touring Lumiadome works on a residency model in which hosts provide flat space, power, and loading access while the operator brings production systems and crew. This is close to the hybrid model and worth studying.

---

## 11. Open questions before a purchase

1. What is the asking price, and who made the dome? (Drives the whole capital plan.)
2. Is the cover translucent, opaque, or blackout, and does it have its flame certificate and label?
3. How much capital is available, and is a phased launch acceptable?
4. Does the team have projection, AV, or event-production experience?
5. Is there a host site in mind for the ticketed season, and in which county?
6. Which customer segment would the business lead with: weddings, corporate, festivals, education, or public shows?
7. Where would the dome and equipment be stored, and is a suitable truck and trailer available?
